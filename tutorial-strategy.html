A few things worth keeping.
A new way to find them.

Teach someone to build a small memory store they actually want to keep. Start with ordinary facts, then add useful structure as the need appears.

What the beginner walks away with

“I can save it my way.
And ask Max for it.”

First a loose detail. Then a list, a picture, a person, or a plan that makes their own day easier.

A loose detailA packing listA photo + storyPeopleA reminder

Two ways to learn

Do it with Max. One instruction at a time, optionally spoken. Save something, explore Collections, and ask for it later. Pause whenever you have what you need.

Look up what to say. A searchable reference for the full capability set, including actions that begin with a tap, a photo selection, or the Share menu.

This is a design workshop, not a working tutorial or a live Max conversation. Example phrases are draft teaching copy grounded in current help; they have not been run through the model in this session.

Teach intent before categories

You can just remember something.

“The spare key is in the blue bowl” needs no list, task, event, or category chosen first. Structure becomes useful when you want to gather things, do something, reserve time, or keep a story together.

Loose memory → a fact

“The spare key is in the blue bowl.”
Ask for it later. No filing exercise first.

List → things together

“Add socks to my Weekend packing list.”
The list name tells Max where it belongs.

Task → something to do

“I need to water the plants.”
Track it and mark it done. No alert yet.

Reminder → a timely alert

“Remind me at 6 PM to water the plants.”
A time and an explicit request for a reminder.

Calendar → reserved time

“Put lunch on my calendar Friday at noon.”
A scheduled appointment.

Memory Event → an experience

“Make an event for my Weekend away.”
Notes and photos grouped as a story.

Photos and people add connections. A picture keeps the scene; your words keep why it matters. A person connects the details you learn about them. These are ways to return to memories, not boxes everyone must fill before Max becomes useful.
Guided learning path

One small success. Then one more.

First save and recall a detail, build a list, and add a photo. Then explore those familiar items in Collections. These five foundation steps can span sessions; the remaining lessons are optional. If someone skips a capture, show only the Collections sections with something in them.

Save & recall→Build a list→Add a photo→Explore Collections→Use your own words

Choose your door: Shopping & packing · Photos · People · Calendar by voice. Someone who came for calendar control can begin there and return to the memory foundation later.

01Foundation

Keep one loose detail

You do not need a folder, a category, or a perfectly worded command.

Pick a real, non-sensitive detail you would otherwise lose. The sample is only a pattern; use an item and location that are true for you.

Try saying
Remember my spare charger is in the top desk drawer.
Then try / do
Where did I put my extra charger?
You keep A useful fact you can ask for later
Completion evidence & recovery

Look for: One saved memory, followed by the correct location. Your question can use different words from the original.

If it goes wrong: Check the save receipt. If the item or location is wrong, correct that exact detail before continuing.

03Foundation

Give a photo a memory

Your words preserve the meaning; visible writing adds another way to find it.

Take a photo inside Max or choose one from your library. Use a label, sign, object, or moment that matters. Optional prepared practice image: the taco shop with its CASH ONLY sign.

Try saying
Remember this taco place. Anna recommended it.
Then try / do

We’ll find this again in Collections after the next capture steps.

Completion evidence & recovery

Look for: The correct photo is attached to the memory. With the provided sample, the answer points to CASH ONLY in the saved photo. A personal photo has its own expected answer.

If it goes wrong: Check that you selected the picture, granted access, and can read the sign in it. A Maps location share does not send a photo. The sample says nothing about a restaurant’s current policy.

04Foundation

Explore it. Add something directly.

Collections is for putting things in as well as getting them out.

You have already saved a loose detail, made a list, and described a photo. Now open Collections from the main screen and check the store label. Start with Lists and Photos, where you have something familiar to find. People, Events, and Tasks can wait until their lessons add something there.

Try in Collections
  1. Open Lists and find the list you made earlier.
  2. Add two useful items directly, without a conversation. Try editing or reordering one.
  3. Open Photos and find the picture you described. Open its associated memory.
  4. Return to the conversation and ask for the list again.
Then ask Max
Show me my Weekend packing list.
You keep A familiar route to the list and photo you already saved
Completion evidence & recovery

Look for: The user opens Collections, adds new items to the existing list directly, and sees those same items when asking Max. Before leaving, have them find the list in Collections again without step-by-step prompting.

If it goes wrong: Check that Collections and the conversation use the same store and the same list name. Offer a cue to the Collections control rather than doing the navigation for them.

For the loose fact from step 1: We do not yet have a good everyday browsing experience for miscellaneous memories. Flip → Your Memories offers inspection, but does not settle that product gap. Recall the loose fact by asking; do not promise that it appears as a Collections item.

A core tutorial skill

Talk to remember.
Open Collections to work with it.

Introduce Collections after capture has put useful things in the store. Finding a familiar list and photo gives the screen meaning. Teach additional sections as the user creates people, events, and tasks.

1 · Find your way in

Open Collections from the main screen. Notice the store label, normally Personal. Start with the sections populated by the preceding lessons.

2 · Do one useful thing

Open the list you created by voice and add two new items directly. Try editing or reordering one. Then find the photo from the earlier lesson.

3 · Hear the connection

Return to the conversation and ask for that list. The direct change is part of the same memory store Max answers from.

Two routes to the same store. The voice-controlled AI assistant is Max’s distinctive strength. Collections is also a primary way to use the app: add items directly, edit, arrange, and manage them whenever that is more convenient. What you enter there becomes part of the same store you can ask Max about. Those direct actions use no conversation turn or AI Assistant allowance; later background filing may have its own usage.

A loose fact still needs no category. Collections is the home for structured views. Browsing miscellaneous memories remains a product gap. Flip → Your Memories can inspect stored facts, but is not yet the approachable everyday view we want. Do not imply every loose fact has its own Collections category.

The companion reference

What can I do?
How can I ask?

Phrases are examples, not magic commands. Replace names, dates, items, and places with your own. Each card says when you need a selection, a permission, or an app control first.

Separate vocabulary from setup. “Remember,” “show me,” “add to my … list,” “remind me,” and “put it on my calendar” teach different intentions. A voice phrase cannot stand in for choosing a photo, connecting a calendar, or approving an invitation.

For the implementation discussion

Make the tutorial easy to leave.
And easy to come back to.

One card at a time

In the app, show one action and one visible goal. Keep Repeat, Previous, Next, and End tutorial in a floating controller. Offer Resume after ending and keep the full reference a tap away.

Use real things

Prefer facts, lists, and photos the user wants to keep. Optional fictional practice data must be labeled, identifiable, and removable without touching their own records.

Let Max conduct

Spoken guidance fits the earlier tutorial direction. Text stays available. The old audio blocker is dated; validate interruption and step timing on the current build.

A floating controller, while you use the app

Keep a compact tutorial controller above the current Max screen as the user moves between conversation and Collections. Max can give the instruction aloud; the controls remain within reach while the person does the actual work.

ControlIntended behavior
RepeatReplay Max’s current tutorial instruction and keep its text available. Do not rerun the save, edit, or other action. Prefer replaying the same instruction without a new model round.
PreviousReturn to the preceding card. Keep the user’s saved work intact; revisiting a card does not undo anything or repeat a completed action.
NextMove to the next card when the user wants. If the current action is unfinished, retain that status; navigation is not proof of completion.
End tutorialStop tutorial narration and dismiss the controller immediately. Keep saved memories and local lesson progress so the user can resume later. Practice-data cleanup is a separate, explicit action.

Placement matters: keep the controller clear of the keyboard, microphone, navigation, and the item the lesson asks the user to tap. Provide a way to collapse or reposition it; support larger text and VoiceOver. Keep the underlying screen usable instead of making the tutorial a blocking dialog.

Speech and navigation: changing cards stops the old instruction before a new one plays. Repeat never starts a second overlapping narration. End tutorial stops tutorial-owned listening as well as narration. Settle automatic advance versus user-controlled Next during testing; neither should interrupt someone mid-action.

Teach both directions.

Voice → Collections: tell Max what to keep, then find and manage it directly.

Collections → voice: add useful items directly, then ask Max to find or answer questions about them.

Tom often adds many items directly in Collections. The tutorial should make that normal, practical usage visible. Success is choosing whichever route fits the moment, not doing everything by voice.

Trial cost is part of the design

Instruction cards, searching the phrase guide, and local progress should require no model call. Real online saves and recalls follow actual conversation usage; cookbook photo reading also has a cost. Help is operator-paid under the existing help design. Whether guided practice receives a separate allowance remains a product decision.

The proposed $0.10 trial budget is a hypothesis, not a measured tutorial allowance. Measure the shortest useful path, its initial round, a repair, and a later return. Record actual metered cost and leave room for personal exploration. Keep optional lessons out of the required path. No paid harness or model calls were run to produce this draft.

Keep one content source, with two presentations

The existing help database should own the capability wording. A future lesson record can reference a help topic, intent, prerequisites, expected outcome, recovery, and a phrase template. The app binds those to its controls and observes the result; the website can show the same story before install. This private draft is a review artifact, not a second production help database.

Items to settle before building
  • First release: use the five-step foundation to build state before exploring Collections; allow pauses and skipped captures. Other entry paths likewise capture something before visiting its Collections section. Keep the phrase reference available immediately.
  • Later product exploration — browsing miscellaneous memories: Tom wants to revisit a better way to see ordinary, uncategorized memories. Consider a recent-memory feed or simple browse/search view in a later design discussion. This tutorial does not assume that view exists; no agent task or app implementation has been started.
  • Practice policy: default to useful real data; decide whether an optional sample pack is worth its cleanup and data-labeling work.
  • Progress: track lesson-created IDs and selected store locally. Existing content can satisfy a lesson; aggregate counts alone cannot.
  • Cost ownership: distinguish operator-paid guidance from actual user interactions. Decide tutorial allowance only after measuring the chosen path.
  • Build audit: verify New Tools recipe entry, calendar provider differences, entitlement gates, and shared-space permissions on the intended trial build.
  • Help conflict: collections.lists.sharing says no collaboration exists; newer shared-store documentation and list-photo help describe shared lists. Reconcile the owning help before publishing that lesson.
  • Tester sessions: try calendar control with Steve, packing and loose memories with Tom, and reminders with a willing caregiver tester. Observe confusion, repair effort, independent recall, whether they return to Collections unaided, later reuse, and cost. No website tracking is introduced here.
