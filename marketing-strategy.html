01
Launch direction

A focused first offer.

Basic stays at $5/month for planning. The adjustable variables are the dollar usage budget per user and Premium packaging. The leading sharing model is Premium creation with paid Basic participation; personal sync remains open.

Tom’s current thinking

Basic at $5 per month

Tom does not expect to change Basic’s price. The strategy is to find a sustainable allowance and a compelling feature package at $5/month. Trial spend and recurring paid-user spend are separate budgets.

A $0.10 trial is a proposal, not a measured sufficient allowance or an implemented entitlement.

Suggested experiment

Release access in batches

Begin with a small cohort, inspect what happened, and decide whether to admit the next one. A proposed first batch of 100 fully used trials costs $10 in model spend.

No decision to launch this experiment has been made. A 1,000-person launch is not required to begin learning.

The central uncertainty

At a fixed $5/month, what usage allowance and feature package make Basic useful enough to keep, while leaving a meaningful reason to choose Premium?

02
Value & audience

Give the promise time to become useful.

Working theory: Max earns trust when something saved earlier becomes useful later. Capture quality, recall quality, and the habit of returning all matter.

“Remember it now. Ask for it later.”

Save something real

A place, a household detail, a client’s preference, or something the person actually expects to forget.

Need it again

The trial should leave room to return days later. A spend limit can coexist with a generous time window.

Recognize the value

Max finds the right thing with little effort. The person has a concrete reason to continue using it.

Formula: trials × allowance = full-use trial spend. Divide that spend by trials × conversion rate to get trial model cost per payer. With zero conversions, no cost-per-payer figure is defined.

What this budget bounds

Only trial model spend, assuming the allowance is enforced. Total exposure also includes infrastructure and any other costs. Implementation must account for an in-flight model round that can cross the remaining balance; a threshold checked only before a round is not a strict ceiling.

What we still need to measure

How far $0.10 goes for a new user: first-session overhead, ordinary saves and recalls, retries, and longer conversations. Historic per-conversation averages do not establish today’s onboarding cost.

The recurring allowance is a separate lever

The calculator above models trial acquisition spend. For a paying user, choose a dollar usage allowance per billing cycle. A larger allowance can support more use, but consumes more of the same $5 subscription revenue.

Evaluate actual spend per paid user, how often people hit the cap, satisfaction, and renewal. The allowance is a ceiling, not assumed average consumption. Sustainable economics must also cover storefront fees and other service costs. No paid-user allocation has been chosen in this notebook.

04
Learning plan · proposed

Buy evidence a little at a time.

Hold Basic at $5. Investigate usage allocations and the placement of sync and shared memories, changing one variable at a time where practical. No experiment below has run.

A

Check what a dime buys

Review representative recent usage or run a separately authorized, budgeted trial simulation. Include saving something, returning for it, and recovering from a misunderstanding.

Decision: Is the allowance sufficient for a useful experience? If it runs out during setup or troubleshooting, improve that path or reconsider the allowance before scaling.

B

Offer one clear proposition to a small cohort

Proposed first batch: 100 trials at $0.10 each. Make the limited trial and subsequent $5/month price clear. Explain the allowance in a way users understand; avoid promising a fixed conversation count until measured.

Decision: Admit another batch only after reviewing spend, useful recall, paid starts, and reasons for leaving. No ad spend or recruitment outreach is authorized by this notebook.

C

Look for voluntary payment and renewal

Keep the cohort’s actual counts: trial starts, paid starts, and renewals among customers who have reached their renewal date. With willing participants, ask whether Max retrieved something useful and what influenced their decision.

Decision: A few payments are encouraging, but repeat use, renewals, and service cost determine whether to expand. Small samples are directional evidence.

Learn without collecting people’s memories.

Use aggregate operational and billing counts where available, plus voluntary feedback. Do not add website analytics, cross-site tracking, or memory-content collection. How to measure useful recall while honoring the product’s privacy stance remains a design question.

Needed before trial launch · draft design

Try it with Max, one small success at a time.

Tom wants a tutorial with predetermined things a trial user can try. Teach interaction through real actions in the app: show one prompt, let the person try it by voice or text, and explain what to look for. Keep the steps predictable while using the person’s own details.

Save a real detail

Prompt: “Remember my spare charger is in the top desk drawer.” Use an actual item and its real location. Show where to speak or type. Look for Max’s save confirmation, and correct it if it heard the wrong thing.

Ask in different words

Prompt: “Where did I put my extra charger?” Adapt it to the item just saved. The lesson: ask naturally; the original wording does not have to be exact. Check that Max returns the saved location.

Check what Max kept

Open the saved memory and review its original words. Show how to edit a mistaken detail or delete a practice memory. Then invite the person to save one useful detail of their own without a suggested sentence.

Steve’s entry point: manage the calendar by voice.

Tom clarified Steve’s motivation: speak to handle his calendar instead of fiddling with the app or switching between apps. The value hypothesis is less interaction effort. Let people choose what brought them to Max at the start. For Steve, lead with the calendar exercise below and offer memory capture afterward. The three-step memory exercise is a default path, not a prerequisite for every user.

Ask Steve which calendar tasks he most wants to do by voice, and watch him try those tasks with his own connected calendar. Note where Max still makes him tap, open another app, repeat himself, or repair a mistake. Judge the complete interaction, including clarification and confirmation, rather than treating voice input alone as success.

Choose one next experience

Offer optional paths after the first success. People can skip, pause, and resume. The full feature tour stays available; finishing every lesson should not be required to use Max.

Suggested sentences are starting points, not required commands. Explain “remember,” “remind me,” “put it on my calendar,” and asking a question where each becomes relevant. A misunderstanding should offer a short correction example and a way to retry or skip, not trap the user in a lesson.

Trial cost and implementation decisions

Keep instructional cards and progress local and deterministic; use real Max interactions for the hands-on steps. Measure the core path, permission recovery, and a retry under current model costs before deciding how much of the proposed $0.10 allowance the tutorial can use. Leave room for personal exploration and a later return. Do not promise a fixed number of turns or hide which activities use the allowance.

Record progress from actual app results where possible; ask the user whether they saw the phone alert. Do not treat reading a card or pressing Next as successful feature use. Keep practice cleanup explicit and user-controlled. Shared-space onboarding can be a separate lesson when a person creates or joins a space.

Evidence to seek: with willing testers, observe where they need help, whether they can save and recall a new detail without the script, whether they return later, and the cost to reach that point. Tutorial completion is distinct from ongoing usefulness and paid conversion. This is a proposed flow; no tutorial or tracking has been implemented.

Central growth hypothesis

One Premium member can bring in paying Basic members.

Tom identified this as a key insight: creating a shared space can give another person a concrete reason to subscribe to Basic at $5/month. Sharing can support both upgrades and customer acquisition.

“Join our household’s memory” gives someone a reason to start.

Premium creates

A Premium subscriber creates a useful shared space and invites the people who belong in it.

Basic joins

An invited person can subscribe to Basic at $5/month to participate. They do not need Premium just to join.

Each member funds their usage

Each person’s subscription carries their own usage allowance. The creator does not automatically fund everyone’s AI conversations.

Leading strategy

Basic is a paid participant

Personal memory, its own usage allowance, and participation in invited shared spaces. The invitation introduces a specific use for Max, potentially making the $5 decision easier.

This is the leading packaging hypothesis, not a change to implemented entitlements. Joining includes the replication needed for a shared space to work.

Premium package

Creation, more usage, possibly sync

Premium can serve people who want to create shared spaces, need a larger usage allowance, or want personal sync if it is reserved for that tier.

Personal sync remains an open tier decision. Premium price, allowances, member limits, trial invitations, and behavior when an owner cancels still need definition.

Illustrative upside: three new Basic members = $15/month.

If one Premium-created space causes three people to become new paying Basic subscribers, they add $15/month in gross subscription revenue, alongside the creator’s subscription. This is a scenario, not a forecast; fees and serving those members reduce the contribution.

Cannibalization is part of the tradeoff

Some guests might otherwise have bought Premium. Others might never have subscribed at all. Compare the value of new, retained Basic subscriptions with any foregone Premium upgrades and the cost of serving the group. An existing Basic subscriber joining a space is not a new acquisition.

What to learnEvidence to seek
Do invitations acquire subscribers?New paid Basic starts attributable to invitations, using available billing information and voluntary feedback. Separate existing customers and trial-only participants.
Does the group keep paying?Renewals among invited subscribers who have reached their renewal date; willing members’ accounts of how they use the space.
Does creation motivate Premium?Customers who choose or upgrade to Premium to create a space, and whether they subsequently invite paying members.
Are the economics sustainable?Actual model and sync costs alongside group subscription proceeds. Each member’s allowance bounds their AI use; shared infrastructure still has costs.

Key signal: new paying members brought in per Premium-created space, followed by their retention and contribution. The cannibalization counterfactual cannot be read from a conversion count alone. Acquisition and retention benefits remain unmeasured.
